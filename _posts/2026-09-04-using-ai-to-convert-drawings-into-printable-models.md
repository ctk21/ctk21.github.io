I wanted to visualise a proposed sliding-door section as a physical scale model, mainly so we could see the sightlines and proportions properly. All I had was a paper drawing showing the horizontal and vertical sections from the door manufacturer, plus a Bambu Lab A1 printer.

I gave ChatGPT a photo of the drawing and asked how I could turn it into a 3D model using free CAD software. Instead, it suggested generating the STL directly.

We first worked on the horizontal cross-section to extrude. The drawing contained several section types and break lines, and ChatGPT was not especially good at interpreting them. I spent about 20–30 minutes correcting the geometry. Once I described changes precisely and in small steps, though, progress was quick, and I had a STL model that could be printed.

That worked well enough that I tried something harder: the plan and section drawings for the surrounding brickwork, including windows, doors and overhangs.

The first result was plausible but wrong in several places: offsets were inverted or ignored, and explaining those errors by referring back to the drawings was slow. The breakthrough was to stop thinking in terms of “plan plus sections” and instead describe a ground-floor footprint with vertical extrusions. Once the overall geometry was right, small, precise iterations worked well.

ChatGPT also split the model into pieces that would fit on the A1's build plate and added keyed joints between them.

I then asked it to reproduce the brickwork. Initially this was slow going with various bricks not aligning between planes or floating across openings. However, when I noticed the STL was being generated by a small Python program using [trimesh](https://trimesh.org/), I decided to alter my approach. Rather than repeatedly pointing out bad geometry, regenerating the STL and checking it, I started asking ChatGPT to inspect the code for the likely cause of each problem and propose a fix.

That was much faster.

More importantly, I now had a *program* for generating the brickwork. Changing from stretcher bond to soldier courses, or trying other variants, became a parameter change rather than a remodel.

The 3D print was the goal, and getting from drawings to the STL I wanted was great (without access to professional CAD tools). The surprise was ending up with generative code derived from the architectural drawings, which made it easy to produce and experiment with variants.
